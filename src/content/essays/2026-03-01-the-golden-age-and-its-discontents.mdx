John Adams died on July 4, 1826, fifty years to the day after the republic he helped found declared its independence from the British Crown. His last recorded words were “Thomas Jefferson survives”. He was wrong. Jefferson had died hours earlier at Monticello, having asked the evening before whether it was the Fourth. Both men had reason to care. They had staked their lives and sacred honor on a proposition about human equality and self-governance that half the civilized world regarded as dangerous nonsense, and the other half as a noble experiment certain to fail. That both departed on the jubilee suggested, to those inclined to see such things, a providential symmetry. The founders of the republic were released from their earthly labors on the very day the nation celebrated what they had built.

Two centuries later, on the evening of February 24, 2026, Donald Trump stood before a joint session of Congress and declared a “golden age” of America. He spoke for over two hours, the longest State of the Union address in at least sixty years, in a chamber where Lincoln’s party once fought to preserve the Union and where Franklin Roosevelt rallied a nation for war. He celebrated military victories abroad, economic transformation at home, and the imminent dawn of America’s semiquincentennial. He awarded the Medal of Honor. He pinned Purple Hearts. He reunited a freed Venezuelan prisoner with his niece on live television. And then, four days after the Supreme Court struck down his tariff authority in a 6-3 ruling that included three of his own appointees, he informed Congress that “Congressional action will not be necessary”.

Between Jefferson’s quill and Trump’s teleprompter lies the entire arc of American self-governance. The distance is not merely temporal. It is constitutional, structural, spiritual. The question that Trump’s second State of the Union forces upon a sympathetic but serious observer is the one Hamilton and Madison debated in the most consequential exchange of political argument in the English language: can a republic possess both executive vigor and constitutional restraint? Hamilton, in Federalist 70, insisted it could. Energy in the executive, he wrote, is “a leading character in the definition of good government”. But Hamilton’s energy was never freelance energy. It was energy channeled through law, directed toward ends that the Constitution enumerated and the legislature authorized. The vigorous executive Hamilton envisioned was vigorous *within* the constitutional frame, not in spite of it. Madison, in Federalist 51, supplied the complementary principle. “Ambition must be made to counteract ambition”. The genius of the design was checked energy: a system of countervailing powers to ensure that no single will, however capable, could substitute itself permanently for the deliberation of the whole.

Trump’s SOTU is Hamiltonian in temperament but not in structure. It celebrates decision. It celebrates action. It celebrates a president who does not wait for legislatures to deliberate, who does not defer to courts when they rule against him, who does not ask permission before ordering American bombers into Iranian airspace or American soldiers into Caracas. The audience is meant to feel the kinetic force of one man acting while others vacillate. And for a great many Americans, exhausted by decades of institutional paralysis and administrative dysfunction, the sensation is exhilarating. But Hamilton’s executive acted with energy precisely so that the constitutional machinery could operate with dispatch. Trump’s executive acts with energy because the constitutional machinery has been allowed to atrophy. The distinction is the whole of the matter.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Four days before Trump addressed Congress, the Supreme Court handed down its ruling in *Learning Resources v. Trump*, striking down the use of the International Emergency Economic Powers Act to impose peacetime tariffs on foreign nations. The decision was 6-3. Justice Gorsuch, a Trump appointee, wrote a concurrence applying the major questions doctrine, the same interpretive principle that constitutional conservatives have championed for years as a check on the administrative state. The irony was not lost on the legal commentariat, though it ought to have been savored rather than weaponized: a conservative Court, armed with conservative legal tools, checked a conservative president. This is the Madisonian architecture working precisely as designed.

But the scandal is not where the commentariat thinks it is.

Within hours, the administration invoked Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 and imposed a 10 percent global tariff. The rate was subsequently threatened to rise to 15 percent on select countries “where appropriate”. The administration signaled its intention to maintain China tariffs at 35 to 50 percent through alternative statutory mechanisms. The European Union froze its trade deal with Washington. FedEx filed suit demanding a full refund on tariffs paid under the now-invalidated IEEPA orders. Congressional Democrats introduced \$175 billion in refund legislation. And Trump told the chamber that Congressional action would not be necessary.

The media treated this as evidence of presidential lawlessness, a strongman improvising around a judicial rebuke. The truth is more damning and more structural. Section 122 is not a loophole that Trump discovered in a dusty statutory drawer. It is a power that Congress wrote into law, published in the Federal Register, and left sitting on the shelf for half a century. Congress drafted the terms. Congress set the trigger (a balance-of-payments emergency, which the administration now claims to have identified). Congress never repealed or narrowed the provision despite decades in which no president invoked it. The same is true of IEEPA itself, and of Section 232, and of Section 301, and of every other statutory grant of trade authority that successive Congresses have piled up since the Trade Act of 1974. Each was written broadly. Each was intended, at the time, for narrow circumstances. None was ever reclaimed.

The real scandal of the tariff sequence is not presidential cleverness. It is legislative self-lobotomy. Congress spent fifty years delegating its Article I commerce power to the executive through one statutory instrument after another, and now expresses shock that a president has used the tools Congress itself manufactured. John Marini spent a career mapping this terrain. Congress, as he demonstrated, voluntarily surrendered its constitutionally enumerated powers to the bureaucracy and to the executive, transforming itself “from primarily a legislative body to an administrative oversight body”. The individual member’s power increased even as the institution’s constitutional function withered. Every broad delegation, every “emergency” provision left on the books long after the original emergency passed, became a weapon in the presidential arsenal. James Burnham predicted Congress would either reassert itself or die. Marini showed it did something worse: it became a handmaiden, exchanging the difficult work of writing general laws for the comfortable work of holding hearings, extracting concessions, and performing outrage on cable news.

Consider the constitutional geometry of the tariff sequence with clear eyes. The Court checked the executive. The executive reached into a statutory armory that Congress built, selected an alternative weapon, and resumed firing. The legislature, which alone possesses the constitutional authority to regulate commerce with foreign nations under Article I, Section 8, was told by the president that its participation was superfluous. And Congress, from both parties, accepted the arrangement. No Republican rushed to reclaim tariff authority. No Democrat proposed a comprehensive trade statute that might supersede executive improvisation. Both parties, for different reasons, prefer a president who acts to a Congress that must legislate. The reason is not mysterious. Legislating on tariffs requires recorded votes. Recorded votes create accountability. Accountability costs elections. Delegation to the executive lets every member of Congress complain about tariff policy without ever taking responsibility for it.

Trump told Congress to its face that its action was unnecessary. Congress applauded. He was right, and they knew it, and that is the constitutional crisis that nobody in Washington has any incentive to name.

The honest conservative position on the substance of trade requires holding two truths simultaneously. Trump’s trade instincts respond to a genuine problem. The industrial hollowing of the American heartland, the strategic dependence on Chinese manufacturing, the persistent imbalances that decades of free-trade orthodoxy failed to resolve: these are real. The voters who sent Trump to Washington understood, with the intuitive clarity that Kesler identified as the strength of healthy populism, that something had gone badly wrong with the global economic order, and that the credentialed class which presided over it had no intention of correcting course. To dismiss the tariff impulse as mere protectionist heresy is to miss the Burkean point: when institutions persistently fail the people they govern, popular anger is not irrationality but a species of political wisdom.

But the method matters as much as the goal. Hayek’s argument in *The Constitution of Liberty* was not merely economic but constitutional. The rule of law requires general rules applied equally to all, not discretionary power exercised case by case at the pleasure of a sovereign will. Trump’s tariff regime is the precise opposite of rule-based trade policy. Different rates for different countries, renegotiated by presidential whim, revised by executive order, struck down by courts, replaced by alternative statutory authority, threatened to be raised further via social media post. This is not free trade. But neither is it principled protection. It is mercantilism by executive decree, and it fails the constitutional test not because tariffs are inherently wrong but because one man deciding, week by week, what each nation pays is the negation of the rule of law.

A uniform tariff, written into statute by Congress after public deliberation, applied equally to all trading partners, and subject to periodic legislative review: this would satisfy both the populist demand for industrial policy and the constitutional demand for legislative supremacy over commerce. That nobody in Washington is proposing anything of the sort tells you everything you need to know. Congress does not want the responsibility. Trump does not want the constraint. The political market that rewards presidents for acting punishes legislators for legislating. And so the republic gets Section 122 workarounds and Truth Social threats, the appearance of policy without the substance of law.

If the tariff drama tests the republic’s commercial constitution, the Iran question tests something graver. War and peace are the highest acts of sovereignty. The Constitution vests the power to declare war in Congress, not because the Founders believed legislatures would make better military decisions than executives, but because they understood that citizens possess an inherent right to participate, through their elected representatives, in decisions that may require them to sacrifice their lives and fortunes. This is not a procedural nicety. The consent of the governed extends above all to the gravest decisions a government can make. A republic that wages war without the deliberate consent of its people’s representatives is a republic in name only, whatever else it may accomplish on the battlefield.

Trump’s SOTU addressed Iran with what, on the surface, appeared to be contradictory language. He lauded Operation Midnight Hammer, the June 2025 strikes that sent seven B-2 bombers to hit Fordow, Natanz, and Isfahan, as having “obliterated” Iran’s nuclear weapons program. Then, having declared the obliteration complete, he warned that Iran was “starting it all over again,” pursuing “sinister nuclear ambitions” and developing missiles that could soon reach the American homeland. He expressed a “preference” for diplomacy while making clear that “one thing is certain”: Iran would never be permitted a nuclear weapon.

The contradiction between “obliterated” and “starting it all over” is not mere presidential incoherence. It is calibrated ambiguity, the kind that experienced statesmen have always employed when addressing both foreign adversaries and domestic audiences simultaneously. Trump is maintaining a casus belli while claiming credit for the first strike. He is keeping Tehran uncertain about American intentions while testing the appetite of Congress and the public for further escalation. The Gang of Eight briefing that Secretary Rubio delivered hours before the speech was the operational reality beneath the declaratory surface: what the public heard in the SOTU was crafted for broad consumption; what the eight congressional leaders heard in the classified session was crafted for decision.

But what the dynamics surrounding the speech revealed was something more troubling than presidential ambiguity.

Senate Minority Leader Schumer emerged from the briefing and told reporters, “This is serious, and the administration has to make its case to the American people”. Parse that formulation carefully. Schumer did not say the administration must seek congressional authorization. He did not invoke the War Powers Resolution. He did not demand a vote. He demanded only that Trump make “his case” more persuasively. This is not opposition to war. It is a demand for better salesmanship. And from the Senate’s most senior Democrat, a man who taunted Trump as “TACO” (Trump Always Chickens Out) when the administration debated striking Iran last June, the formulation amounts to an invitation dressed as a complaint.

The deeper game was laid bare in reporting from independent journalists. A senior Schumer foreign policy aide explained to an official from an organization opposing Iran strikes, in the candor that Washington’s creatures reserve for conversations they assume will remain private, that a substantial number of Senate Democrats believed Iran needed to be dealt with militarily. But those Democrats also understood that another war in the Middle East would be politically catastrophic. Their preferred outcome was for Trump to do it, absorbing the political damage while Democrats collected the strategic benefit. Iran takes a blow and so does Trump. A calculation of breathtaking cynicism, and not one that any participant in the conversation expected to see published.

This is the logic of a legislature that has fully internalized its own irrelevance to the war-making process and now treats the question of force not as a constitutional responsibility but as a political positioning exercise. When the SOTU’s Iran passage earned a bipartisan standing ovation, including from nominally progressive senators like Elizabeth Warren, the applause ratified not a policy but an arrangement: the executive wages war, Congress performs either approval or disapproval depending on the electoral calculus, and the constitutional text that reserves the war power to the legislature gathers dust on the shelf beside Section 122.

Meanwhile, Representatives Khanna and Massie have attempted to introduce a bipartisan war powers resolution to block unauthorized hostilities against Iran. Democratic leadership has actively worked to prevent it from reaching the floor. Two House Democrats, Gottheimer and Moskowitz, staunch allies of Israel, have publicly opposed any constraints on Trump’s military authority. The war powers coalition is not bipartisan in the way that word usually implies. Both parties are undermining it. The institutional dynamic that Marini identified in the tariff context applies with equal force here: Congress prefers to delegate and then complain, because the alternative requires taking a recorded vote on the most consequential decision a government can make, and votes have consequences that speeches do not.

The Venezuela precedent makes the Iran question existential for the constitutional order. Operation Absolute Resolve, the January 2026 capture of President Maduro in Caracas, did generate some War Powers reporting after the fact. The administration filed the paperwork. But the reporting only sharpens the indictment. Congress received notification, introduced war powers resolutions in response, and watched those resolutions die. The constitutional question was settled not by deliberation but by inertia. The institutional failure is not that Congress was left uninformed. The failure is that Congress was informed and still refused to treat authorization as its job. That is worse than an empty inbox, because it indicts the institution itself, not merely the communication protocol. If the authorized capture of a foreign head of state (authorized by the president alone, reported to Congress after the fact, ratified by congressional silence) draws no institutional consequence, then the constitutional settlement on war powers is whatever the president says it is, and Congress is an audience, not a co-equal branch.

Lincoln offers the indispensable comparison, not to flatter Trump or condemn him, but to establish the criteria by which executive prerogative in matters of war ought to be judged. Lincoln suspended habeas corpus, blockaded Southern ports, and expanded the army before Congress convened in the summer of 1861. These were extraordinary measures taken in an actual civil war, with hostile armies in the field and the capital under physical threat. But Lincoln did something that Trump has not done and shows no inclination to do. He submitted his actions to Congress for ratification. In his July 4 message, he acknowledged the constitutional irregularity of what he had done and asked the legislature to validate it retroactively. He did not claim that congressional action was unnecessary. He recognized that it was essential, not for practical reasons but for reasons of principle. The sovereign people, through their representatives, must consent to the exercise of prerogative, or the prerogative becomes indistinguishable from tyranny.

Kesler wrote that “the only populism that could make a difference has at its heart a return to constitutionalism”. The Iran situation is the test of that proposition. If the MAGA movement’s energy is directed solely toward supporting whatever the president decides, without demanding that he seek congressional authorization for major military action, then the movement has settled for something less than constitutionalism. It has settled for executive populism, trusting one man to wield the administrative state’s accumulated powers more wisely than the progressive establishment wielded them. There is still time to prove the critics wrong. But the USS Gerald Ford carrier group is in the Persian Gulf, F-22 Raptors are deployed to Israel, and the diplomatic “deadline” Trump floated publicly is ten to fifteen days. The clock is running, and Congress is watching from the gallery.

One of the minor pleasures of Trump’s SOTU was watching congressional Democrats navigate the theatrical traps he laid for them. The standing-vote gambit on “protecting citizens, not illegal aliens” forced every member to choose, on camera and in real time, between applauding a proposition that polls at roughly 70 percent approval and remaining seated in solidarity with a party leadership that has spent years insisting the distinction is somehow illegitimate. The Democrats who sat delivered, free of charge, precisely the footage that Republican media strategists will deploy in midterm advertising across every competitive district in the country. A more disciplined opposition would have risen, applauded, and deprived the moment of its sting. But discipline requires a coherent political identity, and the Democratic Party does not presently possess one. When your most memorable contribution to the evening is a congressman being escorted from the chamber for waving a sign reading “Black People Aren’t Apes,” you have lost the news cycle before it begins.

Political theater in the modern SOTU is not a Trumpian innovation. It is a bipartisan tradition stretching back decades. Reagan invented the “Lenny Skutnik moment” in 1982. Clinton turned his 1998 address, delivered while the Lewinsky scandal consumed Washington, into a policy performance so masterful that the scandal temporarily receded from public consciousness. Obama was a virtuoso of the emotional vignette. To single out Trump for the practice is to confess either historical ignorance or partisan convenience.

What Trump has contributed is scale. Two hours and change. Medal of Honor, Purple Hearts, Olympic hockey champions, a Venezuelan political prisoner, a waitress from Iowa, a cancer patient, the widow of Charlie Kirk. The speech dissolved into a procession of *exempla*, the classical rhetorical technique in which particular human stories embody universal principles. The technique is as old as Cicero. The density is new: policy became the connective tissue between narratives rather than the reverse. Whether this represents the evolution or the degradation of presidential rhetoric is genuinely debatable. It is certainly more watchable than the technocratic alternative, and it is worth noting that Virginia Governor Abigail Spanberger’s official Democratic rebuttal, delivered by a former CIA officer in the studied cadences of a focus group, accomplished the remarkable feat of being forgotten before it ended.

But the speech’s omissions are as revealing as its performances, and they illuminate the same institutional dynamic that produced the tariff sequence and the war powers abdication.

There is no mention of the national debt. No engagement with the Supreme Court’s reasoning on tariffs beyond the word “unfortunate”. No strategic endgame articulated for Venezuela beyond the claim that America will “run” the country’s oil resources, a formulation that Secretary Rubio contradicted within forty-eight hours of Operation Absolute Resolve and that the SOTU quietly walked back. No explanation of how Operation Midnight Hammer could have “obliterated” Iran’s nuclear program if that program is now described as requiring a massive military buildup and a ten-day ultimatum to address. No mention of the Epstein files, despite revelations creeping closer to uncomfortable proximity. The Stop Insider Trading Act, which Trump proposed to bipartisan applause, produced the evening’s most honest moment: watching members of Congress decide, on camera, whether to clap for a bill that would directly constrain their own financial behavior. The applause was, to put it charitably, uneven.

These omissions are not accidental. They are the product of the same political market that rewards Section 122 workarounds and punishes legislative deliberation. A president who told the truth about the fiscal trajectory, the human costs of potential military escalation, and the constitutional irregularity of executive tariff-making would be rewarded with a primary challenge, hostile coverage, and a one-term presidency. The audience does not want to hear it. Congress does not want to vote on it. The incentive structure of American democracy punishes honesty about tradeoffs and rewards grandiosity. This is not unique to Trump. Obama’s SOTUs were different in tone but identical in function: visions of progress unmarred by the compromises that actual governance requires. The problem is structural, not personal. The same system that allows Congress to delegate its tariff power and then complain about how that power is used also rewards presidents who proclaim golden ages and punishes those who acknowledge constraints.

Burke drew the decisive distinction in his *Reflections* between the revolutionary who promises paradise and the statesman who manages imperfection. Trump’s temperament in this speech was curiously revolutionary despite its conservative content. It promised transformation without acknowledging constraint. But the responsibility is not Trump’s alone. It belongs to a political culture in which the incentives for candor have been systematically destroyed, and in which the institutional actor most constitutionally responsible for imposing fiscal and strategic discipline on the executive, Congress, has spent half a century amputating its own capacity to do so.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Trump closed his address with paeans to liberty, invocations of God, and a vision of national greatness extending into an unbounded future. He spoke of the “flame of liberty” and the “golden age” that his administration was inaugurating on the eve of the nation’s 250th birthday. The rhetoric was sincere in the way that political rhetoric is always sincere: it expressed a genuine sentiment without being encumbered by excessive specificity about what, precisely, the words meant.

Jefferson and Adams, writing to each other in old age after decades of political enmity had given way to philosophical friendship, debated whether the experiment they launched would endure. Jefferson, the optimist, believed in the capacity of an educated citizenry to govern itself. Adams, the skeptic, doubted whether any people could long resist the seductions of faction and ambition. Both were, in their way, correct. The republic has endured far longer than either might have predicted. But the manner of its endurance would have troubled them both.

An executive who governs by decree and dares the other branches to stop him. A legislature that prefers oversight of bureaucracy to the difficult work of lawmaking, and prefers delegation to the painful work of voting. A judiciary that checks presidential power on tariffs but cannot compel Congress to reclaim the authorities it has surrendered. A political market that rewards spectacle and punishes deliberation. And a public that responds to the former with the enthusiasm that self-governing citizens once reserved for the latter.

The 2026 State of the Union was not a crisis. It was a symptom. The constitutional order is not collapsing; it is being slowly, quietly, bipartisanly remade by the accumulated weight of congressional abdication. Trump is the most visible beneficiary of that transformation, and the most candid about exploiting it. He told Congress to its face that its action was unnecessary. Congress applauded. He announced wars that Congress never authorized. Congress stood and clapped. He proposed a bill to ban insider trading by members of Congress. Congress shifted uncomfortably and half-heartedly joined in.

Hamilton wanted energy within a legal frame. Madison wanted that frame jealously guarded by a legislature alert to its own prerogatives. Lincoln, at his greatest, achieved both: decisive action anchored in constitutional purpose, submitted to democratic accountability. The question for the republic at 250 is whether it still possesses the civic virtue, the *prudentia* in the classical sense, to demand the same. Not energy or restraint. Both. Simultaneously. In tension. As the Founders designed.

A golden age proclaimed from a podium is a speech. A golden age written into law, debated by a legislature, checked by courts, and ratified by a self-governing people is a republic. The distance between the two is the measure of the constitutional work that remains.
